# Viraaasat Research 13
## Evidence, Fact-Checking, Research Sources & Editorial Integrity

**Mission:** Build a practical verification system for Viraaasat, a small editorial team publishing immersive, evidence-driven stories about India.

**Editorial principle:** Our credibility must be stronger than our visuals. Accuracy, transparent sourcing, context and corrections outrank speed, spectacle or a predetermined narrative.

---

## 1. The Viraaasat verification standard

Use these rules for every story:

1. **Trace important claims to their origin.** Prefer original datasets, documents, legislation, court records, research papers, transcripts, archival material and first-hand evidence.
2. **Corroborate central claims.** For major factual claims, seek at least two independent sources when feasible. Two outlets repeating one press release are not independent confirmation.
3. **Keep claims in context.** Verify definitions, timeframe, geographic scope, denominator, methodology and what a statistic does *not* show.
4. **Separate fact, interpretation and opinion.** Clearly distinguish documented facts, attributed claims, editorial analysis, uncertainty and forecasts.
5. **Be transparent.** Link and identify the evidence so a reader can retrace our reasoning.
6. **Represent uncertainty honestly.** If a point is unresolved, say so. Do not manufacture certainty to make the narrative cleaner.
7. **Correct openly.** Record and publish meaningful corrections promptly, clearly and proportionately.
8. **Preserve evidence.** Keep notes, source links, downloads, relevant excerpts, dates, calculations and verification decisions in the story's research record.

These principles are consistent with published standards from Reuters, BBC, AP and the International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN), while adapted for Viraaasat's scale and format.

---

## 2. Source hierarchy — a guide, not an automatic ranking

A source's category does not make it infallible. A primary source is often the best evidence for *what it records or says*, but may not be the best source for evaluating its own performance or motives.

### Tier A — Primary evidence
- Original government datasets and statistical tables
- Laws, rules, Gazette notifications, parliamentary proceedings, court judgments
- Original reports, audits, budget documents and administrative records
- Peer-reviewed research and original scientific datasets
- Direct interviews and first-hand observations
- Original archival documents, maps, photographs, film and audio
- Official records from relevant institutions

**Use:** Establish what the original evidence records. Check date, scope, definitions and methodology.

### Tier B — Expert and independent analysis
- Academic researchers and subject experts
- Independent research institutes with transparent methods
- Specialist publications that cite their evidence
- Independent audit, evaluation or field studies

**Use:** Interpret primary evidence, explain methods and provide context. Assess funding, institutional interests and limitations.

### Tier C — Established reporting
- Reputable national, regional and specialist news organizations
- Reporting with named sources, transparent methods and editorial standards
- Investigations that publish documents or explain evidence

**Use:** Find leads, context, interviews, local knowledge and corroboration. Trace their important claims back to underlying sources when possible.

### Tier D — Leads and discovery sources
- Social media posts, forums, personal blogs, viral videos
- Wikipedia and other encyclopedias
- Search snippets, AI summaries, aggregators
- Unsourced infographics and forwarded messages

**Use:** Discover leads or understand what people are discussing—not as final evidence for consequential claims without verification.

### Important exception
For a source that is the sole authoritative record of a specific event or fact, explain why it is the only available source. Do not invent a second source simply to satisfy a checklist.

## 3. Source evaluation checklist

For every source, assess:

- **Authority:** Who produced it? What expertise or access do they have?
- **Accuracy:** Can the claim be checked against underlying evidence?
- **Relevance:** Does it directly address our question?
- **Currency:** Is it the correct period/version?
- **Transparency:** Are methods, definitions and data disclosed?
- **Purpose:** Inform, advocate, sell, defend, persuade or entertain?
- **Independence:** Is it independent of other sources being cited?
- **Limitations:** What does it omit or fail to measure?
- **Conflict of interest:** Funding, institutional, political, commercial or personal interests?

A source with a conflict of interest may still contain valid information; disclose and independently test its claims rather than dismissing or accepting it automatically.

## 4. Claim ledger — required research artifact

Maintain a claim ledger for every story. This is the story's factual backbone.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Claim ID | Stable identifier (e.g. C-001) |
| Claim | Exact factual statement we intend to publish |
| Importance | Core / supporting / background |
| Claim type | Statistic / historical / quote / legal / scientific / descriptive / allegation / forecast |
| Primary source | Original evidence, link, page/table/section |
| Secondary source(s) | Independent corroboration and context |
| Source date | Publication date and date accessed |
| Scope | Geography, population, timeframe, definition |
| Verification | What was checked and how |
| Caveat | Uncertainty, limitations, exceptions |
| Story placement | Scene, paragraph, caption, chart, interaction |
| Status | Unverified / in progress / verified / disputed / rejected |
| Reviewer | Teammate who reviewed it |
| Notes | Calculation, interpretation, unresolved issues |

### Claim statuses
- **Unverified:** Not yet checked.
- **In progress:** Evidence gathered; verification incomplete.
- **Verified:** Evidence supports the precise wording and context.
- **Disputed:** Credible evidence conflicts or remains unresolved.
- **Rejected:** Evidence does not support the claim, or it is too weak to publish.

Do not label a whole story “fact-checked” when individual important claims have different levels of confidence.

## 5. Verification by claim type

### Statistics and data
Check:
- Original dataset and release
- Definitions and units
- Denominator and population
- Geographic coverage
- Time period and comparison periods
- Collection method and sample
- Missingness, revisions and limitations
- Whether a rate, total or absolute count is being used appropriately
- Whether the chart implies a causal relationship that the data cannot establish

**Rule:** A statistic is not publication-ready until its context is understandable to a reader.

### Historical claims
Check:
- Primary documents, inscriptions, contemporary accounts, archival sources
- Date, location, translation and provenance
- Scholarly interpretations and disagreements
- What is established, inferred or contested
- Whether modern labels are being projected onto historical people or events

Do not present a contested interpretation as settled fact. Explain disagreement fairly and with enough context for the reader.

### Quotes and interviews
Check:
- Exact wording against recording, transcript or contemporaneous notes
- Speaker identity and role
- Context before and after the quote
- Permission and agreed terms, where applicable
- Translation and transliteration, if applicable

Never silently change a quote's meaning for dramatic effect.

### Scientific and technical claims
Check:
- Original research, paper, dataset or technical documentation
- Study design, sample, peer review and limitations
- Whether a result is replicated or preliminary
- Difference between correlation and causation
- Whether expert interpretation matches the strength of evidence

### Legal, political and policy claims
Check:
- Current primary legal text, order, judgment, notification or official record
- Status: proposal, bill, enacted law, rule, court stay, implementation or policy announcement
- Jurisdiction and effective date
- Independent context where claims about impact or performance are made

Avoid simplifying procedural status in a way that changes meaning.

### Allegations and claims about individuals or organizations
- Seek documentary evidence and independent corroboration.
- Attribute claims precisely.
- Distinguish allegation from established finding.
- Seek response from the subject when reasonably practicable and appropriate.
- Record outreach, timing and response or non-response.
- Escalate potentially defamatory, serious, sensitive or high-risk material for additional review before publication.

### Visuals and user-generated content
For photos, videos, maps and social posts:
- Confirm original creator/source and rights/permission
- Verify location, date and context
- Reverse-search images where appropriate
- Check landmarks, signage, weather, language and other contextual clues
- Check for edits or manipulation where relevant
- Don't treat a caption or repost as proof
- Don't use illustrative or reconstructed visuals in a way that implies they are documentary evidence
- Label archive, illustration, simulation and reconstruction clearly

For high-stakes visual verification, AP describes checks including reverse-image search, geolocation, contextual comparison, frame-level manipulation review and contacting the original uploader.

## 6. Data integrity and visualization checks

Before publishing any chart, map, timeline or interactive:

### Data provenance
- [ ] Original dataset/source identified
- [ ] Source URL and access date saved
- [ ] Dataset/version saved where possible
- [ ] License/usage terms checked
- [ ] Definitions, units and coverage recorded

### Transformations
- [ ] Cleaning steps documented
- [ ] Exclusions and missing values documented
- [ ] Calculations independently checked
- [ ] Aggregations and conversions reproducible
- [ ] Any estimates or approximations clearly labelled

### Visual representation
- [ ] Chart type fits the question
- [ ] Axes and units are clear
- [ ] Scale and baseline do not mislead
- [ ] Geographic boundaries and projections appropriate
- [ ] Color is not the only encoding
- [ ] Comparison periods are equivalent
- [ ] Labels, legends and source notes are visible
- [ ] Static/text alternative is available
- [ ] Mobile view preserves meaning

### Narrative integrity
- [ ] Visual evidence supports the surrounding prose
- [ ] Animation does not exaggerate change or causality
- [ ] No misleading cherry-picked range or framing
- [ ] Important caveats remain visible
- [ ] The reader can inspect the data or source

## 7. India-specific research approach

India-related research frequently crosses institutions, languages, state jurisdictions, datasets and historical interpretations. Use the following discovery paths, then verify the specific claim from the source:

### Government and public records
Potential starting points include:
- Ministry and department portals
- Parliament questions, debates and committee reports
- State government notifications and department reports
- Gazette of India and state gazettes
- CAG reports
- Court websites and judgments
- Census and survey documentation
- National statistical datasets
- Public data portals

### Research and academic sources
- Universities and institutional repositories
- Peer-reviewed journals
- Research centres and field studies
- Working papers (label their status)
- Books from reputable publishers
- Archaeological and archival institutions

### Local and lived evidence
- Interviews with residents, workers, practitioners and affected communities
- Local reporting
- Regional-language publications
- Oral histories and community archives

**Translation principle:** For translated sources, preserve the original where possible, identify the translation method, and have a fluent speaker review meaning-critical passages. Avoid flattening culturally specific terms into approximate English.

**Coverage principle:** A national story must not assume one state, city, caste, language, religion, class or region represents the whole country.

## 8. Avoiding misleading narratives

A story can use technically correct facts and still mislead.

### Watch for
- Cherry-picking the best or worst period
- Comparing unlike populations or regions
- Confusing correlation with causation
- Using a percentage without its denominator
- Using absolute numbers without per-capita context
- Outdated figures presented as current
- Confusing announcements with implementation
- Treating a single anecdote as representative
- Presenting only the evidence that supports the story's premise
- Using a map or chart that visually exaggerates differences
- False balance: giving unsupported claims the same weight as strong evidence
- Loaded wording or emotional framing that prejudges the conclusion
- Overconfident language when evidence is uncertain

### Editorial counter-question
Before finalizing a story, ask:

> **What evidence would make us change our mind about the central framing?**

If the team cannot answer, the story may be advocacy disguised as investigation.

## 9. Fairness and right of reply

For significant criticism of a person, institution, company, government body or identifiable group:

1. Identify exactly what is being alleged or criticized.
2. Confirm the supporting evidence.
3. Give the subject a reasonable opportunity to respond when appropriate.
4. Ask clear, specific questions—not a vague request for comment.
5. Record when and how the request was sent.
6. Include the substantive response fairly.
7. If there is no response by deadline, state that accurately without implying guilt.
8. Keep correspondence in the research record.
9. Escalate high-risk material for editorial/legal review.

The absence of a response does not confirm an allegation.

## 10. Corrections policy

Corrections are a visible part of trust, not an embarrassment to hide.

### Suggested correction levels

**Level 1 — Minor**
Typographical or formatting error that does not affect meaning. Correct directly.

**Level 2 — Factual clarification**
A detail was inaccurate, incomplete or ambiguous. Update the relevant passage and note the correction where readers can see it.

**Level 3 — Significant factual error**
A central claim, number, interpretation or visual was materially wrong. Correct prominently, date the correction, explain what changed and why.

**Level 4 — Story integrity issue**
A story's central premise or important evidence is unreliable. Pause promotion, investigate, seek editorial review, and consider a prominent correction, substantial rewrite or withdrawal. Preserve a transparent record of the decision.

### Correction note template
> **Correction — [date]:** An earlier version of this story [briefly describe the error]. It has been corrected to [correct information]. We regret the error.

Never silently change a major error and pretend the original was correct.

## 11. Sources section and reader transparency

Each published story should have a source section that is useful—not a decorative list of links.

For important sources, include:
- Source title
- Author/organization
- Publication date (when available)
- Direct link to the evidence
- Relevant report, dataset, table, page or section
- Optional short note on what the source supports

For data-heavy stories, add a methodology note explaining:
- Data origin and version
- Time period and coverage
- Definitions
- Cleaning or transformation
- Limitations
- What the visualization represents

Readers should be able to follow the trail from a major claim back to evidence.

## 12. Research record structure

Suggested per-story research folder:

```text
stories/
└── story-slug/
    ├── brief.md
    ├── outline.md
    ├── draft.mdx
    ├── claims.md
    ├── sources.md
    ├── methodology.md
    ├── interviews/
    │   └── notes.md
    ├── data/
    │   ├── original/
    │   ├── processed/
    │   └── README.md
    ├── assets/
    │   └── rights-register.md
    └── review/
        └── editorial-checklist.md
```

Only retain records that are useful and lawful to keep. Protect sensitive interview notes, private contact details and information that could put a source at risk. Do not publish confidential research material accidentally.

## 13. A practical review workflow for a team of three

### Researcher / story author
- Build the claim ledger
- Collect source evidence
- Draft source notes and methodology
- Mark uncertainty
- Prepare the story narrative

### Independent reviewer
- Verify central claims and selected supporting claims
- Check source independence and context
- Challenge the framing and look for contrary evidence
- Review calculations and visuals

### Builder / visual editor
- Validate that visuals accurately represent the evidence
- Check labels, caveats, interactions and static alternatives
- Review source links and asset provenance
- Perform final publication QA

Roles can rotate. For consequential claims, the author should not be the only person approving their own evidence.

## 14. Editorial sign-off checklist

### Story readiness
- [ ] The central question is clear.
- [ ] The premise is supported, not assumed.
- [ ] Important claims are in the claim ledger.
- [ ] Central claims have strong evidence and corroboration where feasible.
- [ ] Contradictory or inconvenient evidence was considered.
- [ ] Opinion, interpretation, allegation and fact are distinguishable.
- [ ] Context and uncertainty are disclosed.
- [ ] Sensitive claims received appropriate review.

### Data and visuals
- [ ] Data source and methodology documented.
- [ ] Calculations and transformations checked.
- [ ] Charts/maps/timelines do not distort meaning.
- [ ] Visual assets are verified, credited and rights-cleared.
- [ ] Reconstruction or illustration is labelled.
- [ ] Accessible alternative exists.

### Publishing
- [ ] Sources are linked.
- [ ] Methodology notes are ready where needed.
- [ ] Right of reply was handled where appropriate.
- [ ] Correction process is in place.
- [ ] Teammate review completed.
- [ ] Final version and source records preserved.

## 15. Proposed public editorial promise

A possible short statement for Viraaasat's eventual About / Editorial Standards page:

> Viraaasat aims to tell stories about India with curiosity, rigor and openness. We seek primary evidence, corroborate important claims, distinguish fact from interpretation, and provide context for data and visuals. We acknowledge uncertainty, disclose relevant limitations and correct significant errors transparently. Our stories are designed to explore—not to force a predetermined conclusion.

This is a proposed editorial commitment, not a claim of formal certification or membership in a fact-checking organization.

## Final takeaway

**Viraaasat should be visually ambitious but epistemically humble.**

We can create dramatic reveals, cinematic transitions, immersive maps, emotional photography and interactive data—but the reader should always be able to see what is evidence, what is interpretation, what is uncertain, and where it came from.

**A beautiful story can attract attention. A transparent story earns trust.**
