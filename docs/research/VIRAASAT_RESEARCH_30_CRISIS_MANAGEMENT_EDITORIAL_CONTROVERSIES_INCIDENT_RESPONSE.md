# Viraaasat Research 30
## Crisis Management, Reputation, Editorial Controversies & Incident Response
**Research date:** 22 November 2026  
**Scope:** Three-person independent India-focused digital editorial publication  
**Status:** Operational recommendations; legal and security incidents may require professional advice

---

## Executive summary

A crisis is not only a viral controversy. For Viraaasat it can include publishing a factual error, receiving a credible legal complaint, misusing an image, exposing reader data, a security incident, targeted harassment, threats, impersonation, or an interaction breaking during a high-traffic story.

The core recommendation is **prepare a response system, not a reaction script**.

Principles:
1. **Pause and assess before replying.** Fast is not the same as impulsive.
2. **Protect people first.** Staff, contributors, sources, readers, and anyone affected by the story.
3. **Preserve evidence.** Don't delete material or messages before documenting them.
4. **Separate the problem types.** Accuracy issue, disagreement, harassment, security breach, legal threat, and operational outage need different responses.
5. **Assign one incident lead and one outward spokesperson.** Small team still needs role clarity.
6. **Say what is known, what isn't, and what happens next.**
7. **Correct substantive mistakes transparently.**
8. **Don't feed harassment.** Avoid prolonged arguments with coordinated attackers.
9. **Do not expose private information in the name of transparency.**
10. **Review and learn after the event.**

These principles are consistent with newsroom online-abuse protocols from the International Press Institute (IPI), Reuters Institute, CPJ, IWMF, PEN America, and UNESCO-related journalist safety resources. Protocols must be adapted to a small team's capacity and context.

## 1. Incident taxonomy

| Incident | Examples | First priority |
|---|---|---|
| Editorial error | Wrong figure, misleading quote, wrong date/place | Verify and correct |
| Context/framing criticism | Story omits a perspective, misleading visual, insensitive language | Review the substance; consult relevant expertise |
| Bias accusation | Reader claims ideological or regional bias | Evaluate against evidence and editorial standards |
| Coordinated backlash | Mass mentions, brigading, spam, threats | Staff safety, moderation, evidence preservation |
| Personal harassment/doxxing | Home address or personal details posted | Immediate safety and digital-risk assessment |
| Credible threat | Threat of violence, stalking, physical targeting | Safety, emergency support, appropriate authorities |
| Legal complaint | Defamation, privacy, copyright, notice from lawyer | Preserve records; legal review |
| Copyright/permission dispute | Rights holder challenges use | Verify rights; assess removal/temporary restriction |
| Security incident | Account takeover, leaked credentials, site defacement | Contain, secure, investigate, recover |
| Data incident | Submission/newsletter data exposed | Contain; assess impact and legal obligations |
| Technical outage | Story or interactive fails, hosting unavailable | Restore accessible version; communicate if useful |
| Impersonation | Fake Viraaasat account or staff identity | Document, report, warn readers if risk |

## 2. Three-person incident roles

Use the team structure as roles—not permanent job titles. Rotate, but name who is responsible in advance.

### Incident Lead
- Opens the incident log
- Determines severity and scope
- Assigns tasks and checkpoints
- Decides whether publication should be paused, hidden, or amended
- Ensures team members are not acting independently or contradicting each other

### Editorial Reviewer
- Rechecks claims, sources, framing, headline, visuals, and affected context
- Determines whether the complaint is valid, partly valid, or unsupported
- Leads corrections and editorial decisions

### Communications & Safety Lead
- Monitors official contact channels and relevant public conversation
- Drafts external response for review
- Preserves evidence of harassment/threats
- Protects staff personal information and accounts
- Avoids escalating through reactive replies

For a small team, one person can wear multiple hats. For high-risk incidents, all three should review decisions and seek outside help where appropriate.

## 3. Severity levels and escalation

### Level 0 — Routine feedback
Examples: disagreement, criticism, typo reports, questions.

**Response:** review through normal editorial process. No crisis label or public response needed.

### Level 1 — Material editorial concern
Examples: a disputed figure, incomplete context, inaccurate caption, misleading graphic.

**Response:** assign reviewer; recheck sources and affected presentation. Correct if warranted. Keep a record.

### Level 2 — Sustained public criticism or coordinated harassment
Examples: rapid volume of hostile messages, coordinated posting, targeted accusations, attempts to identify staff.

**Response:** incident lead; staff safety check; document evidence; reduce direct engagement; secure accounts; moderate official channels; consider a concise statement if factual information is needed.

### Level 3 — Credible threat, doxxing, serious legal issue, or security/data incident
Examples: credible threat of violence, address published, account compromise, confidential source exposure, formal legal notice, serious personal data exposure.

**Response:** immediate safety/security action; preserve evidence; contact appropriate emergency services, platform, security professionals, or legal counsel as appropriate; restrict compromised access; consider pausing publication or affected functionality. Do not delay urgent safety action while waiting for team consensus.

## 4. First 15 minutes: initial response checklist

1. **Stop reactive posting.** Don't quote-post, argue, or publish an unreviewed statement.
2. **Identify what is happening.** Error? criticism? harassment? threat? legal notice? security incident?
3. **Check people.** Are team members, sources, contributors, or family exposed or at risk?
4. **Preserve evidence.** Screenshots, links, timestamps, emails, headers, relevant article versions; store securely.
5. **Secure the channel if necessary.** If account/device compromise is suspected, use a trusted device to secure credentials and sessions.
6. **Name the incident lead.**
7. **Set a next review time.** Avoid endless monitoring by everyone.
8. **Escalate if credible danger, data breach, or serious legal issue.**

The time window is a practical team target, not a universal emergency-response SLA.

## 5. The response decision tree

### A. Is the criticism about content accuracy or framing?
- Yes → assess the specific claim; re-open sources; identify whether it affects the story's conclusion or reader understanding.
- If correct → fix it and publish an appropriate correction/update.
- If partly correct → clarify, correct affected portions, explain nuance.
- If unsupported → document the review; respond only if a response would help.

### B. Is it harassment or coordinated abuse?
- Preserve evidence.
- Don't engage with individual harassers.
- Block, mute, filter, report as appropriate.
- Assess physical and psychological risk.
- Consider whether public support or statement would help or increase risk.

### C. Is there a credible threat or private information exposed?
- Treat as a safety incident.
- Do not publicly repeat the private information.
- Preserve evidence securely.
- Tell affected people and coordinate support.
- Seek appropriate authorities or professional help if warranted.

### D. Is it a legal notice or credible rights claim?
- Preserve the notice, relevant drafts, source records, and publication versions.
- Avoid admissions or aggressive counterclaims.
- Refer to qualified legal counsel.
- Consider temporary restriction only as a deliberate, documented decision—never as an automatic response to every complaint.

### E. Is it a security or data incident?
- Contain access or exposure.
- Revoke compromised sessions/keys; rotate credentials as appropriate.
- Identify affected systems/data.
- Preserve logs and evidence.
- Assess applicable legal and notification obligations with counsel.
- Restore from trusted backups and verify.

## 6. Correction and accountability protocol

### Correction workflow
1. Receive and log the concern.
2. Acknowledge it where contact details exist.
3. Recheck original sources and underlying evidence.
4. Ask a second reviewer for material claims.
5. Decide: no change / clarification / correction / withdrawal or temporary restriction.
6. Make changes.
7. Add a visible, dated correction note for material errors.
8. Notify the complainant of the outcome if appropriate.
9. Update internal claim ledger and correction log.
10. Review how the error entered the workflow.

### Correction language examples

**Minor correction (if no correction note is warranted):** Fix a typo or formatting error that does not alter meaning.

**Material correction:**
> **Correction — [date]:** An earlier version of this story stated [incorrect information]. The correct information is [correct information]. We have updated the story.

**Clarification:**
> **Clarification — [date]:** This section has been updated to explain [missing context]. The original wording could have suggested [misinterpretation].

**Update:**
> **Update — [date]:** Since publication, [new development]. This update is separate from the original reporting and does not change [relevant distinction].

Do not use a correction note to minimize or obscure a serious error.

## 7. Responding to accusations of bias

Use an editorial audit, not a defensive reflex.

Review:
- What claim is being challenged?
- Does the story use the same evidentiary standards across actors?
- Are sources selected fairly and represented accurately?
- Does a headline imply something the story doesn't support?
- Is an image or visualization editorially loaded?
- Is context missing?
- Does the story present uncertainty accurately?
- Is there a meaningful counterargument or response that belongs?
- Is criticism based on disagreement with facts, an actual flaw, or both?

### Decision outcomes
- **Valid criticism:** acknowledge, correct, and explain.
- **Partly valid:** fix the issue and clarify the remaining evidence.
- **Substantive disagreement:** explain the method/evidence briefly, if useful.
- **Bad-faith or abusive:** don't engage; use moderation and safety tools.
- **Unclear:** review further; avoid premature statement.

Do not reflexively label criticism as trolling, politically motivated, or coordinated without evidence.

## 8. Public statements: when and how

A public statement is useful only if it serves a purpose.

### Consider issuing one when:
- Readers need an accurate status update.
- A material correction or editorial decision needs explanation.
- A fake account is impersonating the publication.
- There is a safety issue readers can help avoid.
- A coordinated campaign has created factual confusion about what the publication did.

### Avoid statements when:
- It would amplify a minor complaint.
- You are still investigating and have no verified information.
- It repeats harmful claims or private information.
- It is mainly an emotional response.
- It invites a prolonged argument.

### Statement principles
- One official channel.
- Short, plain, factual.
- No insults, sarcasm, speculation, or counter-accusations.
- Don't reveal sources, private information, or security details.
- Include what is known, what is being reviewed, and when readers can expect an update if appropriate.
- Review by the incident lead and editorial reviewer.
- Affected staff should be consulted about statements that concern them.

### Templates

**Review in progress**
> We are aware of concerns about [story/content]. We are reviewing the specific issues and underlying sources. We will update the story or explain our findings when that review is complete.

**Correction made**
> We reviewed the concern about [specific issue] and found that [brief explanation]. We have corrected [specific item] and added a correction note to the story. Thank you for bringing it to our attention.

**No change after review**
> We reviewed the concern about [specific issue], including [briefly describe evidence/method]. We have not found evidence supporting the proposed change. We remain open to specific new evidence or context.

**Safety statement**
> We are aware of abusive/threatening messages directed at our team. We are documenting and reporting them, and are taking steps to protect those affected. We will not share personal information or engage with threats publicly.

These are starting templates; customize carefully and don't use them to disclose unnecessary details.

## 9. Online harassment, doxxing, and threats

### Evidence preservation
Save:
- Screenshot including account name and visible timestamp
- Original URL/post/message
- Relevant platform and account identifiers
- Context needed to understand the threat
- Dates and chronology
- Any follow-up contact

Store evidence in a restricted folder; avoid circulating it broadly within the team.

### Protect staff
- Don't use personal contact details as public editorial contacts.
- Enable strong MFA/passkeys where available.
- Use unique passwords and password manager.
- Keep recovery codes secure.
- Review account sessions and connected apps.
- Protect home/family/location information.
- Avoid engaging with attackers.
- Use block/mute/report features and filters.
- Assess whether public response could escalate.
- Tell a trusted person/team member; don't handle threats alone.
- Seek professional emotional support if needed.

If a threat appears credible or immediate, prioritize personal safety and contact local emergency services or trusted authorities. Do not attempt to investigate or confront the threat actor.

### Doxxing
If personal data is posted:
1. Don't repeat the information publicly.
2. Capture evidence securely.
3. Report the content to the platform.
4. Secure personal accounts and devices.
5. Assess physical risk, including family and associates.
6. Consider professional support and authorities if risk is credible.
7. Review what information can be removed from public profiles and data sources.

## 10. Legal complaints and threats of litigation

A legal threat is not automatically valid; nor is it automatically frivolous.

### Protocol
- Save the original communication and delivery details.
- Don't delete relevant material.
- Do not publicly post the complaint or mock the claimant.
- Avoid admissions, promises, or deadlines in an impulsive reply.
- Notify the incident lead.
- Seek Indian legal counsel for serious allegations or formal notices.
- Review publication, sources, drafts, permissions, responses, and chronology.
- Follow counsel's advice about response, correction, preservation, or restriction.

A rights complaint involving an image, video, map, or document should be routed to the media rights ledger and reviewed promptly.

## 11. Security or privacy incident

### Containment
- Disable/restrict compromised account, integration, or feature.
- Revoke active sessions and rotate affected credentials/secrets.
- If compromise is suspected, use a clean/trusted device for recovery.
- Restrict access to affected data.
- Preserve logs, evidence, and backups before cleanup where practical.

### Assessment
- What was accessed or exposed?
- Which people/data are affected?
- How long was exposure possible?
- Was source information or sensitive material exposed?
- Are the systems still compromised?
- What notification obligations may apply under current law/contracts?

### Recovery
- Restore from known-good state.
- Verify integrity and access.
- Monitor for recurrence.
- Communicate accurately to affected people and authorities where required.
- Document decisions and lessons learned.

Do not assume that deleting the visible artifact ends the incident.

---

## 12. Technical outage or story failure

For a broken interactive or outage:
- Keep a readable, static version of the story if possible.
- Remove or disable broken interactive modules without hiding the story's core information.
- Provide an accessible text/table alternative for maps and data.
- Avoid blocking the first viewport with a loader.
- Deploy a known-good rollback if a release creates a serious problem.
- Communicate only if readers are affected or need a status.
- After recovery, check mobile, browser, keyboard, reduced-motion, and network performance.

A technical incident should not become an editorial emergency if the story can remain readable in a static fallback.

## 13. Team communication protocol

Use one internal incident channel/thread and one incident record.

Record:
- Incident ID
- Start time
- Incident lead
- Type and severity
- What is verified
- What is unknown
- Actions taken
- Decisions and rationale
- Who is responsible
- Next checkpoint
- Outcome
- Lessons learned

### Team rules
- No parallel public statements.
- No speculation in team chats.
- Don't forward threats widely.
- Don't blame the person who reported the incident.
- No one is required to personally engage attackers.
- The author is included in editorial decisions but does not have to manage the crisis alone.
- Team safety and mental health are legitimate incident priorities.

---

## 14. Post-incident review

Within a reasonable period after stabilization, conduct a short review—not a blame session.

Ask:
1. What happened?
2. What did we know, and when?
3. What was the impact?
4. What worked?
5. What failed or delayed us?
6. Did anyone feel unsupported or unsafe?
7. Did our response amplify the problem?
8. What process, security, editorial, or communication change is needed?
9. Who owns the change?
10. When will we revisit it?

Keep the review proportionate. The goal is to learn and improve.

## 15. Pre-crisis preparation checklist

### Editorial
- [ ] Corrections policy published and understood.
- [ ] Source and claim ledger kept for each story.
- [ ] High-risk stories identified before publication.
- [ ] Headlines, social posts, captions, and visuals reviewed with the story.
- [ ] Response-to-criticism decision process agreed.

### Security
- [ ] MFA/passkeys enabled on email, GitHub, domain registrar, hosting, and social accounts.
- [ ] Recovery codes stored safely.
- [ ] Shared accounts avoided where individual access is possible.
- [ ] Personal contact and home information minimized publicly.
- [ ] Backup and recovery process tested.
- [ ] Incident log ready.
- [ ] Team knows how to report threats.
- [ ] Security contact/provider details available.

### Communications
- [ ] Official spokesperson selected per incident.
- [ ] Shared editorial contact route published.
- [ ] Statement templates stored.
- [ ] Social account moderation controls known.
- [ ] Decision rules for when not to respond agreed.

### People
- [ ] Team members know they can step away from harassment.
- [ ] Threats can be reported confidentially.
- [ ] Support contacts identified.
- [ ] No public response without review when an incident is active.

## 16. Viraaasat-specific recommendations

1. **Do not make all three founders monitor criticism continuously.** Rotate one person on duty and schedule checks.
2. **Never respond to viral criticism in the heat of the moment.** Use the incident lead/reviewer system.
3. **If a story is wrong, correct it clearly and promptly.** Don't defend the indefensible.
4. **If criticism is legitimate but doesn't require changing the conclusion, fix the flaw and explain the method calmly.**
5. **If criticism is abusive, prioritize safety and moderation over winning an argument.**
6. **Don't quote or repost harassing content just to show the audience how bad it is.** This can amplify it.
7. **Don't publish staff personal details, source identities, or private submissions as part of a rebuttal.**
8. **Keep a prewritten incident checklist—not a prewritten apology for every situation.**
9. **High-risk stories should have a pre-publication risk assessment and a plan for response.**
10. **A mistake is an event to repair, not an identity crisis for the publication.**

## Bottom line

Viraaasat should not confuse reputation management with editorial integrity.

If we are criticized, we investigate. If we are wrong, we correct. If we are right but misunderstood, we explain—without arrogance. If someone is abusive or threatening, we protect our people instead of feeding the attack. If something breaks, we restore it and learn.

**The goal is not to never have a controversy. The goal is to be a publication that can respond with evidence, care, restraint, and consistency when one inevitably arrives.**
