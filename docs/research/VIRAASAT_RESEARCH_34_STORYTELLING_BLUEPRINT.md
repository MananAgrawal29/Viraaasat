## Executive summary

Viraaasat should author stories as **sequences of meaningful editorial scenes**, not long pages decorated with scroll effects.

A useful narrative loop:

**Scene → Question → Evidence → Visual explanation → Pause → Discovery**

Scroll is a pacing mechanism. The reader remains in control, and each scene earns its place by helping them feel, understand, question, or discover something.

The most useful research conclusion is that scrollytelling is a format—not a substitute for narrative. The story's question, evidence, audience, and subject determine the visual treatment.

---

## 1. Research findings: what strong scrollytelling is

### Scroll is the pacing control
Scrollytelling supports a guided sequence while letting readers control the pace through scrolling. It is most useful when sequence matters and the reader benefits from progressively revealing a complex idea.

### Technique families
Visual journalism commonly uses:
- Graphic sequences that build an explanation.
- Animated transitions that connect visual states.
- Panning and zooming to establish context or scale.
- Scroll-controlled video sequences.
- Progressive reveal or animated content when it helps comprehension.

Use only the techniques the story needs.

### Separate editorial decisions from technical effects
A chapter decides what the reader should understand or feel. The display mechanism (sticky visual, image sequence, chart, map, video, 3D) and trigger (scroll threshold, progress, step change) are implementation choices. Do not let a favorite animation dictate the story.

### Guided narrative followed by exploration
For data-heavy or complex stories, consider a guided narrative followed by an open exploration section. Readers get the core explanation first, then can explore deeper if they choose.

### Breathing room matters
Major visual transitions should be separated by calm reading passages. A static section is a deliberate pause, not a failure of animation.

### Accessibility and performance are structural
The complete story should remain understandable when motion, video, JavaScript, or heavy media are unavailable. Scrollytelling should be readable, mobile-tested, and not dependent on hidden-only content.

## 2. Viraaasat storytelling principles

### Principle A — One chapter, one question
Every chapter should have a clear question or purpose. Examples (illustrative only):
- What are we looking at?
- How did this come to be?
- What does the evidence show?
- Why does it vary?
- Who is affected?
- What remains uncertain?
- What could change?

### Principle B — Evidence precedes spectacle
Introduce the evidence or mechanism, then choose a visual form. The visual must not dramatize beyond what the evidence supports.

### Principle C — A visual state is a unit of meaning
Document each scene with:
- Reader question / chapter purpose.
- Key insight.
- Text anchor.
- Evidence / sources.
- Visual state.
- Interaction, if any.
- Motion, if any.
- Static alternative.
- Mobile adaptation.
- Accessibility requirements.
- Exit / transition.

### Principle D — Scroll is reversible
Readers must be able to scroll back without breaking the scene, losing context, or encountering abrupt jumps. Use stable transitions; avoid irreversible or forced sequences.

### Principle E — Interaction is optional depth
The narrative should still communicate its core message if readers never touch a control. Optional exploration deepens understanding; it does not gate essential information.

### Principle F — Rhythm is contrast
Alternate visual intensity:
- full-bleed imagery;
- calm text;
- diagram;
- data;
- human story;
- pause;
- synthesis.

Do not make every chapter fullscreen or interactive.

### Principle G — Text and visual tell the same story
If a visual carries key evidence, the text should explain the insight. If a chart/map changes, provide labels, context, and an equivalent accessible explanation.

## 3. A topic-agnostic story architecture

This is a flexible framework, not a prescribed story template. A story may skip, reorder, combine, or invent chapters.

### Possible narrative movements

| Movement | Possible reader question | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Why should I care or be curious? | Establish a scene, tension, question, or mystery |
| Orientation | What am I looking at? | Give context without overloading |
| Discovery | What is surprising or misunderstood? | Reveal a mechanism, evidence, history, or pattern |
| Context | How does this fit into a wider system? | Establish scale, chronology, geography, or relationships |
| Complexity | What complicates the first impression? | Show tensions, counterevidence, unevenness, or uncertainty |
| Human connection | What does this mean in lived experience? | Connect systems to people, places, and consequences |
| Possibility / implication | What follows from this? | Examine options, consequences, or unresolved questions |
| Synthesis | What should I now see differently? | Offer an earned conclusion, not a forced moral |
| Accountability | How do we know? | Sources, methods, limits, credits, corrections |

Not every story needs all movements. Story-specific editorial judgment remains central.

## 4. Story development: blueprint before animation

For any story the team chooses, follow this order:

1. **Story brief:** subject, central question, intended audience, reader takeaway, scope, and risk.
2. **Claim ledger:** list important claims, evidence needed, sources, uncertainties, and what would falsify or qualify the claim.
3. **Narrative outline:** chapter questions and sequence—without motion decisions.
4. **Storyboard:** each chapter's text, visual, evidence, and reader transition.
5. **Visual proof:** rough still frames or sketches. Test if the narrative works without animation.
6. **Motion pass:** add motion only where it clarifies scale, cause, sequence, transition, or attention.
7. **Prototype:** build a small sample of the story to validate production cost and interaction quality.
8. **Review:** editorial, data, rights, mobile, accessibility, and performance QA.

Do not skip from idea directly to effects.

## 5. Chapter / scene contract

Each chapter should be documented before implementation.

### Reusable scene fields

- **ID and title**
- **Question:** What does this scene answer?
- **Key insight:** What should the reader remember?
- **Evidence:** Claim IDs, sources, data, interviews, and limitations.
- **Text:** Narrative copy or approved draft.
- **Visual:** Photograph, illustration, map, chart, diagram, video, or visual state.
- **Interaction:** What can the reader do, if anything?
- **Motion:** What changes and why?
- **Trigger:** How is the state activated?
- **Still state:** What it looks like when motion is absent.
- **Mobile adaptation**
- **Accessibility:** Keyboard, screen-reader text, alternatives, reduced motion.
- **Exit / transition:** How the reader proceeds.
- **Production notes:** Assets, rights, dependencies, and QA.

### Illustrative scene contract (generic)

**Scene title:** A system revealed

- Question: What is happening beyond what we can initially see?
- Key insight: A visual explanation makes a hidden relationship easier to understand.
- Evidence: The sources supporting the explanation, including uncertainty.
- Visual: A layered diagram or sequence.
- Interaction: Optional tap-to-explain labels.
- Motion: Progressive reveal of the layers.
- Trigger: Scroll progression.
- Static state: Fully labeled diagram.
- Mobile: Diagram simplified and labels moved into a readable sequence.
- Reduced motion: Show all layers and labels without animation.
- Exit: Move to the next chapter question.

This example is generic and is not a recommendation for any specific Story 01 topic.

## 6. Visual rhythm

Avoid continuous maximalism. Use a varied sequence as a starting point:

| Scene type | Visual intensity | Reader mode |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | High | Observe / feel curiosity |
| Orientation text | Low | Read / understand |
| Reveal or visual explanation | High | Understand spatially or conceptually |
| Explanation | Low | Learn |
| Sequence / mechanism | Medium | Follow |
| Data or comparison | High | Interpret |
| Quiet paragraph | Low | Pause |
| Exploration | High | Discover |
| Human or contextual section | Medium | Connect |
| Synthesis | Low | Reflect |

This is conceptual guidance, not a fixed storyboard. The rhythm must follow the subject and evidence.

**Rule of thumb:** every intense visual scene should earn a quieter passage. Readers need space to process.

## 7. Motion choreography: what to use and what to avoid

### Useful patterns
- Mask or clip reveal.
- Layer reveal.
- Graphic sequence.
- Sticky visual with changing text.
- Progressive chart.
- Gentle pan or zoom.
- Chapter transition / visual cut.
- Pause or static state.
- Horizontal rail only where content genuinely benefits.

### Avoid
- Constant parallax on every image.
- Heavy scrubbed video sequences without a clear explanatory purpose.
- Heavy 3D merely for spectacle.
- Autoplay audio.
- Scroll-jacking or forced scroll speed.
- Repeated entrance animations on every paragraph.
- Endless pinned sections.
- Essential text appearing only during animation.
- Interaction overload.

### Motion implementation direction
- CSS for small transitions.
- Native sticky and Intersection Observer for simple scene changes.
- GSAP + ScrollTrigger for complex pinned, scrubbed, or sequenced visuals.
- Avoid introducing a new library solely for one story.

## 8. Reader orientation and navigation

The reader should always know:
- where they are in the story;
- approximately how much remains;
- how to continue or pause;
- how to return to previous sections;
- how to access sources.

Recommended:
- restrained reading-progress indicator;
- compact chapter navigation on desktop where useful;
- mobile-friendly chapter list or section marker;
- visible chapter headings;
- no compulsory autoplay or automatic scrolling.

For long stories, chapter names and progress indicators help without overwhelming the immersive design.

---

## 9. Mobile composition

Mobile is a separate editorial composition, not a scaled-down desktop.

- Shorter opening where appropriate.
- Avoid large desktop-only pinned scenes.
- Diagrams should be vertically readable and labels must not collide.
- Maps should support tap selection and clear textual alternatives.
- Data graphics must be legible without pinch-zoom.
- Interaction targets should be comfortable.
- Avoid horizontal overflow unless intentionally controlled.
- Reduce motion complexity and duration.
- Test on a real phone and lower-powered hardware.
- Story remains complete in static and reduced-motion modes.

---

## 10. Accessibility and progressive storytelling

Every scene has a static, accessible state.

### Required fallback
- Text exists in the document.
- Images have useful alt text or are decorative.
- Maps and charts have a text summary or table.
- Controls are keyboard-operable.
- Focus is visible and not trapped.
- Motion respects `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Video has captions and/or transcript.
- Audio is opt-in and has transcript.
- Core content does not depend on hover.
- Content is not hidden when JavaScript fails.

### Progressive layers
1. Core story text and headings.
2. Static images, diagrams, and data.
3. Basic interactive controls.
4. Scroll choreography.
5. Optional heavy media or 3D.

The story remains readable at layer 1 and complete by layer 2.

## 11. Length and complexity guidance

These are flexible production estimates, not editorial quotas:

- Narrative text: roughly 1,500–3,000 words for a medium-depth immersive story, adjusted to the material.
- Chapters: roughly 6–10, but shorter or longer is fine.
- Major interactive modules: aim for 2–5 meaningful modules rather than many decorative interactions.
- Video: only if it adds reporting or visual evidence.
- 3D: only when spatial understanding genuinely benefits.
- Reading time: estimate based on text and optional exploration; don't artificially inflate it through pinned scroll distance.

A concise story is not inferior. Length should be earned.

## 12. Team workflow for a three-person team

Roles may flex across the team, but define ownership for each phase.

### Editorial / research
- Central question and claim ledger.
- Narrative draft.
- Source review and fact-check.
- Reporting contacts.

### Visual / experience
- Visual direction and storyboard.
- Image selection and media rights.
- Map/chart/diagram design.
- Mobile composition.

### Technical / production
- Story architecture and interactions.
- Motion and performance.
- Responsive/accessibility implementation.
- QA and deployment prep.

All three review the final narrative, especially evidence, visuals, and interpretation.

---

## 13. Storyboard review gate

Before production, answer:

- Can the story be summarized in one sentence?
- Does every chapter answer a distinct question?
- Is the sequence necessary?
- Does each interaction explain something?
- Are claims linked to evidence?
- Does the story work without motion?
- Are visual states understandable when paused?
- Does mobile feel intentionally composed?
- Can the team realistically build and QA it?
- Does the ending offer synthesis rather than sermon?

If any answer is “no,” revise before implementation.

---

## 14. Final recommendation

Use an **author-guided, chapter-based visual story that gradually opens into optional exploration**.

The storytelling approach is:

**Scene → Question → Evidence → Visual explanation → Pause → Discovery.**

For each story, create a bespoke narrative and storyboard. The framework should help us work consistently—but must never dictate what India story we choose, what angle we take, or what conclusion we reach.
