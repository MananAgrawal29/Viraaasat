# Viraaasat Research 22 — Editorial Strategy, Story Selection & India's Topic Map

**Mission:** Build a durable editorial strategy for choosing stories, mapping India's breadth, avoiding repetitive coverage and metro-centric bias, and maintaining a long-term story backlog.

**Project:** Viraaasat — *India, in all its complexity.*

---

## 1. Executive conclusion

Viraaasat should not behave like a news feed or an encyclopedia. Its editorial product is **understanding**: a story should help a reader see a familiar or unfamiliar part of India differently.

Build an editorial map (the subject universe) and a story-selection system (the decision mechanism), but do not turn either into a rigid publishing formula. A topic map is a guardrail against blind spots—not a quota that forces weak stories.

### The central editorial question

> **What does this story help a reader understand about India that they couldn't easily understand before?**

If a pitch cannot answer that, it may be an interesting post, but it may not yet be a Viraaasat story.

---

## 2. Editorial identity: the Viraaasat lens

The publication should remain curious, evidence-driven, candid, nuanced, and emotionally intelligent. It should not become a predictable celebration page, outrage machine, political commentary feed, textbook, tourism guide, or statistics dump.

### Six editorial promises

1. **Complexity over convenient conclusions** — hold contradiction; do not force a neat moral.
2. **Evidence before conviction** — claims should be traceable, contextualized, and proportionate to evidence.
3. **People before abstraction** — systems and statistics should connect to lived experience wherever appropriate.
4. **Specificity over generic India imagery** — a place, person, object, institution, practice, or data point should make the story concrete.
5. **Plurality over a single national voice** — India is not represented by one city, language, class, caste, region, religion, age group, or experience.
6. **Curiosity over certainty** — a story can leave the reader with better questions, not just a verdict.

### The story should try to do at least one of these
- Reveal something hidden or overlooked.
- Explain a system that shapes everyday life.
- Reframe a familiar assumption.
- Connect past and present.
- Show a meaningful contrast or contradiction.
- Make a place, process, or dataset understandable.
- Introduce a human experience that complicates a larger narrative.
- Show how something works, fails, changes, or might change.

## 3. India's editorial map — subject universe

Use this as a planning taxonomy. Stories can cross categories; each story should have one primary home for tracking and one or more secondary tags.

### A. History & memory

- Ancient and early histories—beyond familiar dynasties and monuments
- Medieval and regional histories
- Colonialism, resistance, reform, and collaboration
- Independence and partition: personal, regional, institutional, and long-term effects
- Princely states, borders, migration, and state formation
- Forgotten people, movements, places, and inventions
- History of everyday objects, foods, professions, institutions, and infrastructure
- Oral histories and local memory
- Archaeology, archives, contested interpretation, and historical myth
- How historical narratives are constructed, preserved, or omitted

**Story lenses:** What is remembered? Who remembers it? What evidence survives? What changed in the retelling?

### B. Culture, language & identity

- India's languages, scripts, dialects, and language politics
- Literature, poetry, oral storytelling, publishing, and reading cultures
- Music traditions, contemporary music scenes, instruments, and sound cultures
- Cinema, television, theatre, animation, and regional industries
- Festivals, rituals, faith practices, and changing traditions
- Food, agriculture-to-plate systems, regional cuisines, and food memory
- Craft, textiles, materials, artisans, and changing livelihoods
- Clothing, design, architecture, and aesthetics
- Cultural exchange, hybridity, migration, and diasporas
- Internet culture, memes, creator ecosystems, and youth culture

**Avoid:** treating culture as a static catalogue of colorful traditions. Investigate economics, power, adaptation, disagreement, and contemporary life.

### C. Geography, land & environment

- Himalaya, plains, deserts, plateaus, coasts, islands, forests, wetlands
- Rivers, groundwater, watersheds, floods, droughts, and water conflicts
- Monsoon: its science, unpredictability, infrastructure, and consequences
- Biodiversity, wildlife corridors, conservation, and human-wildlife coexistence
- Land use, mining, deforestation, erosion, and changing landscapes
- Climate change: heat, sea-level risk, extreme rainfall, agricultural change
- Cities and their ecological footprints
- Environmental restoration, adaptation, and community-led stewardship
- Geological stories: rocks, soils, fossils, tectonics, and India's deep time
- How geography shapes culture, economy, politics, and everyday behavior


### D. Society, people & everyday life

- Caste, social mobility, discrimination, and structural inequalities
- Gender, masculinity, femininity, family, care, and changing roles
- Childhood, adolescence, education, and aspirations
- Ageing, elder care, disability, accessibility, and inclusion
- Migration, remittances, displacement, and belonging
- Rural life—without romanticization or treating villages as uniform
- Urban life—beyond a few metropolitan centers
- Housing, informal settlements, public space, sanitation, and daily infrastructure
- Work, domestic labor, informal economy, and dignity
- Marriage, relationships, friendship, loneliness, and social change
- Communities and identities; representation must be specific, ethical, and contextual

**Story lens:** How does a large social idea actually show up in a person's day, choices, opportunities, or constraints?

### E. Economy, work & livelihoods

- India's macroeconomy explained through everyday experience
- Household finances, consumption, savings, debt, and financial inclusion
- Informal work, gig work, platform work, and labor protections
- Farmers, farm labor, agricultural markets, crop choices, and risk
- Small businesses, MSMEs, manufacturing, and supply chains
- Industrial clusters, regional economies, and trade
- Jobs, wages, skill, unemployment, underemployment, and labor transitions
- Banking, credit, fintech, insurance, and digital payments
- Inequality, wealth, opportunity, and economic mobility
- Public spending, budgets, taxation, subsidies, and state finances
- What growth looks like in different states, districts, households, and sectors

**Avoid:** using GDP as the whole story. Explain definitions, base years, inflation, denominators, distribution, and limits.

### F. Politics, institutions & civic life

- How Parliament, state legislatures, courts, local government, and regulators work
- Elections, representation, constituencies, campaign systems, and voter behavior
- Public policy: the design, implementation, intended outcome, and unintended effects
- Federalism, state capacity, municipal governance, and decentralization
- Rights, law, constitutional questions, and civic institutions
- Bureaucracy and the everyday citizen-state interface
- Political finance, accountability, public procurement, and transparency
- Public services: who receives them, who is excluded, and why
- Policy debates explained without assuming readers already know the acronyms
- Political history and institutional change

**Guardrails:** distinguish fact, legal status, claim, allegation, analysis, and opinion. Give relevant parties fair representation; verify sensitive claims independently.

### G. Science, technology & innovation

- Indian science history and contemporary research
- Space, astronomy, physics, biology, mathematics, and earth science
- Public research institutions, laboratories, universities, and scientists
- Technology used in ordinary life: UPI, telecom, identity systems, digital public infrastructure
- AI, robotics, automation, and their real-world consequences
- Engineering and indigenous innovation
- Medical research, public health systems, and evidence-based explainers
- Technology adoption: who benefits, who is left out, and what tradeoffs emerge
- Failed experiments, uncertainty, scientific disputes, and research limitations
- Explaining complex ideas through interactive visual storytelling

**Avoid:** treating announcements, prototypes, or projections as proven outcomes. Explain what exists, what is demonstrated, what is promised, and what remains uncertain.

### H. Infrastructure & systems

- Railways, roads, ports, airports, logistics, and freight
- Electricity, grids, renewables, storage, and energy transition
- Water supply, irrigation, sewage, waste, and drainage
- Housing, construction, urban planning, and public spaces
- Telecom, internet, connectivity, and digital infrastructure
- Public transport, walking, cycling, accessibility, and road safety
- Infrastructure financing, land acquisition, maintenance, and governance
- How systems are built—and what it takes to keep them working
- Infrastructure in small towns, remote areas, border regions, and underserved places

**Story lens:** follow a system end-to-end. A map of a road or a statistic about capacity is only a beginning; investigate use, reliability, costs, effects, and maintenance.

### I. Health, food & wellbeing

- Public health, primary care, hospitals, and health access
- Nutrition, food security, malnutrition, and changing diets
- Maternal, reproductive, child, and adolescent health
- Mental health, stigma, and access to support
- Disease, prevention, epidemiology, and health misinformation
- Health workers, caregivers, and health system realities
- Traditional practices—studied carefully and distinguished from scientific evidence
- Aging, disability, rehabilitation, and long-term care
- Environmental determinants of health: air, water, heat, sanitation

**Guardrails:** medical claims need high-quality evidence, careful language, and clear separation between population findings and individual advice.

### J. Education, knowledge & opportunity

- Government, private, and alternative schooling systems
- Higher education, colleges, universities, and research capacity
- Coaching, competitive exams, and the education economy
- Vocational learning, apprenticeships, skill development, and employment transitions
- Literacy, language, digital access, and learning inequality
- Teacher experiences, classroom conditions, and pedagogy
- Student life, aspiration, pressure, migration, and first-generation learners
- How opportunity differs across place, income, gender, disability, and language
- Indian knowledge systems and modern scholarship—neither dismissive nor uncritical

### K. Cities, villages & regional India

This is a cross-cutting geography lens rather than a single topic.

- Tier 1 cities—but beyond familiar lifestyle narratives
- Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities, towns, district headquarters, and smaller urban centers
- Village clusters, rural towns, and changing settlement patterns
- Industrial, border, coastal, hill, forest, and pilgrimage towns
- City systems: housing, transport, water, waste, markets, public space
- Regional migration and the relationship between hometown and destination
- Local histories and local futures
- The same issue viewed across different regions or settlement types

### L. Sports, play & competition

- Popular and less-covered sports
- Sporting infrastructure and access
- Athlete pathways, training, funding, and support
- Women's sport and para-sport
- Local games, regional sporting cultures, and school/community sport
- Sports economics, fandom, identity, media, and commercialization
- The science of performance, recovery, training, and competition

### M. Art, design & creative industries

- Visual art, photography, sculpture, installation, and public art
- Architecture and design across eras and regions
- Independent music, film, theatre, animation, and creative communities
- Publishing, comics, illustration, gaming, and digital creativity
- Creative labor, patronage, markets, and access
- How Indian creators influence and are influenced by global culture
- Preservation, restoration, experimentation, and contemporary reinterpretation

### N. Religion, philosophy & belief

- Philosophical traditions and texts in context
- Religious practices, institutions, pilgrimages, and communities
- Faith and everyday life
- Interfaith contact, coexistence, conflict, and pluralism
- Religion in art, architecture, music, law, politics, and public life
- Modern interpretations and changing practice

**Guardrails:** avoid treating any faith or community as monolithic; distinguish theology, practice, institutions, individual belief, and political mobilization.

### O. Future, change & possibility

- Demographic shifts, future work, and aging
- Climate futures and adaptation
- Future cities and mobility
- AI, automation, and technological change
- India's role in global systems and geopolitics
- Emerging science and infrastructure
- Youth aspirations and future anxieties
- Possible futures: evidence-based scenarios, not prophecy
- What is changing slowly, quickly, or unevenly—and why

### P. The overlooked / unclassifiable

Keep a permanent shelf for stories that don't fit existing categories:

- An unusual object with a surprising history
- A small local innovation
- A place with a hidden system
- A contradiction that resists categorization
- A person who changes how a subject is understood
- A question discovered during research
- A story that connects several otherwise separate categories

This shelf is essential. The editorial taxonomy must not become a cage.

## 4. Cross-cutting lenses: how to make the map genuinely Indian

Subject categories alone will not prevent narrow coverage. Every quarter, review the backlog and published stories through these lenses.

### Geographic lens

- North / South / East / West / Central / Northeast (recognizing these are broad and imperfect categories)
- Himalayan, plains, desert, coastal, island, forest, plateau, border regions
- State, district, city, small town, village, and non-settlement geographies
- Avoid treating the Northeast, Kashmir, tribal regions, borderlands, or islands as occasional exotic exceptions

### Social lens

- Income and class
- Caste and social structure
- Gender and sexuality (handled with care, privacy, and appropriate sourcing)
- Age: children, youth, working age, older adults
- Disability and accessibility
- Language and literacy
- Religion and community
- Rural/urban and migration status

### Time lens

- Ancient / medieval / early modern / colonial / post-independence / contemporary
- Slow change vs sudden change
- Generational change
- Long-run historical patterns and short-run policy effects

### Evidence lens

- Firsthand observation and interviews
- Official datasets and documents
- Peer-reviewed research
- Historical and archival sources
- Expert explanation
- Visual evidence and geospatial analysis
- Data limitations and conflicting evidence

These lenses are planning prompts, not identity quotas. Do not publish a story solely to fill a box; use them to notice omissions and challenge habitual choices.

## 5. Story types: vary the editorial form

Viraaasat should not publish the same kind of story every week. Maintain a mix of narrative forms.

1. **Explainer** — demystifies a system, concept, policy, or process.
2. **Deep dive** — investigates a question with evidence, context, and multiple layers.
3. **Human story** — follows an individual, family, community, or occupation; avoids reducing a person to an illustration of a thesis.
4. **Place story** — a specific location becomes a lens into a broader issue.
5. **Data story** — reveals a pattern through carefully sourced data and visual explanation.
6. **Historical investigation** — traces evidence, memory, interpretation, and consequence.
7. **Comparative story** — contrasts places, eras, systems, or experiences fairly.
8. **How-it-works** — shows the mechanics behind a visible phenomenon.
9. **Myth vs evidence** — examines a popular belief without humiliating the audience or oversimplifying.
10. **Profile** — an individual or institution is explored through context and scrutiny, not promotional framing.
11. **Visual essay** — images, captions, and sequencing carry the narrative, with sourcing and context.
12. **Interactive investigation** — a map, timeline, simulation, or comparison is necessary to understand the story, not merely added for spectacle.
13. **Solutions / response story** — examines a response to a problem, its evidence, limitations, replicability, and tradeoffs; avoid success-story PR.
14. **Question-led story** — starts with a question that research genuinely investigates and may leave partially unresolved.


## 6. Story-selection rubric

Use a simple scorecard to compare pitches—not to mechanically select the highest score. Rate each factor **1–5**.

| Criterion | What it asks | Weight |
|---|---|---:|
| Reader understanding | Will the story materially improve understanding? | ×3 |
| Originality / overlookedness | Is the angle fresh, underexplored, or meaningfully reframed? | ×2 |
| Evidence strength | Can the central claims be supported with reliable evidence? | ×3 |
| Human / cultural significance | Does it matter to people's lives, identity, systems, or shared context? | ×2 |
| Visual / interactive potential | Is the chosen form genuinely suited to visuals or interaction? | ×1 |
| Editorial fit | Does it express Viraaasat's identity and approach? | ×2 |
| Feasibility | Can the team produce it to a high standard with current capacity? | ×2 |
| Timeliness or evergreen value | Is there a meaningful now—or long-term relevance? | ×1 |

**Weighted score:** sum of rating × weight. Maximum = 80.

### Suggested interpretation (initial hypothesis; calibrate after several pitches)
- **65–80:** strong candidate; likely commission if scope and evidence are sound.
- **52–64:** promising; improve the angle, sourcing, or scope before commissioning.
- **40–51:** hold for a sharper question or stronger evidence.
- **Below 40:** probably not a fit right now.

### Non-negotiable gates (not scored)
A pitch should not advance if:
- Its central premise is unsupported or misleading.
- The team cannot responsibly verify sensitive claims.
- The story requires media/assets that cannot be cleared and has no viable alternative.
- The likely treatment risks harm, stereotyping, or privacy without a justified, mitigated public-interest purpose.
- The scope is not realistically deliverable and cannot be reduced without breaking the story.

A low score is not a permanent rejection; a pitch can be revised, resourced, or revisited.

## 7. The story pitch card

A pitch should fit on one short card before becoming a full brief.

- **Working title**
- **One-sentence central question**
- **Why should someone care?** (one sentence)
- **What might the reader understand by the end?**
- **Why this angle, why now?**
- **Evidence plan:** 3–5 likely primary/reliable sources or reporting routes
- **Human/place anchor:** if relevant; not mandatory for every story
- **Visual / interactive idea:** what form might make the explanation clearer?
- **Category + cross-cutting tags**
- **Story tier:** Editorial / Interactive / Flagship (see Research 21)
- **Risks / unknowns**
- **Estimated effort and owner**
- **Next action**

Avoid prematurely designing the page before the question, evidence, and narrative are understood.

## 8. Coverage balance dashboard

Review this every 8–12 stories, or quarterly—whichever comes first. The purpose is to identify patterns and omissions, not to force balance by the numbers.

Track at least:

- Primary subject category
- Secondary category tags
- Story type
- Region/state/settlement type (when applicable)
- Time period
- Main evidence type (data, archive, interview, research, observation, etc.)
- Story tier
- Positive / negative / mixed / neutral framing (descriptive internal tag; do not reduce stories to emotional buckets)
- Timely / evergreen
- Topic and audience familiarity (known / overlooked / complex / emerging)

### Warning patterns to look for
- Too many stories from the same metropolitan cities
- Too much national-level abstraction and not enough state/district/local specificity
- Too much “India is amazing” or “India is broken” framing
- Repeatedly covering the same policy, community, or cultural subject
- Stories about marginalized communities only when there is suffering or conflict
- Northeast, islands, borderlands, rural areas, small towns, or ecological regions treated as rare novelty
- Too many data-led stories without people or context
- Too many human profiles without explaining the system
- History stories dominated by a narrow period or a few familiar empires
- Science coverage restricted to space or technology announcements
- Stories that look interactive but offer little insight
- Lack of stories about ordinary life, joy, ingenuity, humor, care, or ambiguity

### Suggested internal visualizations
- Category matrix over time
- Map of story locations (avoid false precision for sensitive stories)
- Story type × category matrix
- Tier and effort trend
- Region and settlement-type distribution
- Evergreen vs timely timeline
- “Coverage gaps” list derived from tags

Don't publish a coverage score as if it proves editorial fairness. It's a diagnostic.


## 9. Story backlog strategy

Maintain three shelves—not one giant list.

### Shelf A — Evergreen foundations
Stories that remain useful for years:
- How India's federal system works in practice
- How the monsoon works—and what happens when it changes
- How Indian railways move people and goods
- What a census can and cannot tell us
- India's languages and how they evolve
- Groundwater: where it comes from, how it is used, and why it matters
- How India's informal economy works
- How a city gets water
- How public health systems detect and respond to outbreaks
- The history of a common everyday object or institution

### Shelf B — Timely windows
A developing event, policy, court decision, election, scientific finding, weather/climate event, cultural moment, or public debate can create a reason to publish now. Timely does not mean rushed: apply the same evidence and editorial gates.

### Shelf C — Long-lead / flagship
Stories requiring original fieldwork, complex datasets, archival research, specialized visual design, 3D, or substantial reporting. These are planned ahead and should not be forced into weekly release slots.

### Backlog health
- Keep ideas separate from commissioned stories.
- Every commissioned story has an owner and next action.
- Avoid overcommitting: only one active build, one next story, and one release candidate when possible (Research 21).
- Keep a “revisit later” shelf with a reason to revisit.
- Record why a pitch was declined or deferred, briefly, to avoid losing promising ideas.

## 10. Long-term planning: the editorial horizon

Think in rolling horizons rather than an annual list that becomes stale.

- **Next 4 stories:** concrete and production-ready; balance format, subject, and effort.
- **Next 12 stories:** establish diversity across categories, regions, story types, and tiers.
- **Next 30–50 ideas:** broad exploration, low commitment; include weird, local, technical, historical, and human stories.
- **Flagship horizon:** 3–6 ambitious stories that can take several weeks or months.

### A useful recurring planning question

> If a reader followed Viraaasat for six months, what would they learn—and what would still be missing?

This is more useful than trying to make each individual story represent “all of India.”

## 11. Sample starter backlog (angles, not final commissions)

These are deliberately angle-first, and are not claims that research is already completed.

### History & memory
1. **The history beneath a street name** — what naming reveals, and whose memory it preserves or replaces.
2. **A forgotten industrial town** — how an industry shaped its landscape, people, and afterlife.
3. **The everyday object with a long Indian history** — trace a material, tool, or household object through time.

### Geography & environment
4. **Where the monsoon begins for a place—and what the forecast misses** — local geography, rain, and lived consequences.
5. **The disappearing wetland at the edge of a city** — ecological role, land pressure, community, and policy.
6. **A river's journey through one district** — connect watershed, livelihoods, infrastructure, and pollution without pretending one river story explains all.

### Society & everyday life
7. **The first-generation college student** — navigate aspiration, costs, family, language, and opportunity without making a single person stand in for everyone.
8. **Who keeps a city running before sunrise?** — workers and systems that become invisible when the city wakes.
9. **What does accessibility look like in an Indian public space?** — compare design, rules, lived experience, and implementation.

### Economy & work
10. **A product's journey through India's informal economy** — choose one commodity and trace the people and systems behind it.
11. **The economics of a small shop** — rent, margins, credit, digital payments, family labor, and local competition.
12. **What happens after a crop leaves the farm?** — logistics, market prices, storage, processing, and risk.

### Politics & institutions
13. **What does a municipal corporation actually do?** — follow one service from budget to street.
14. **How a law becomes everyday reality** — trace a policy from legislative text through implementation and citizen experience.
15. **The power of a district** — map the district administration's role, limits, and interface with citizens.

### Science & technology
16. **How UPI moves a payment** — infrastructure, participants, reliability, and what the system doesn't solve.
17. **India's heat problem, explained from the body outward** — science, built environment, work, health, and mitigation.
18. **A research lab's long road from idea to use** — realistic timelines, translation barriers, and uncertainty.

### Infrastructure & systems
19. **The journey of water to one home** — source, treatment, network, intermittency, storage, and governance.
20. **How a railway station is choreographed** — people, signaling, timetables, maintenance, and movement.
21. **A road after it is built** — maintenance, safety, drainage, traffic, and the actual experience of use.

### Culture & language
22. **A language at the border of two regions** — identity, borrowing, migration, and daily speech.
23. **A craft's changing economics** — materials, skill, markets, design, and future generations.
24. **A song's journey across India** — migration, film, radio, remix, and regional identity; rights clearance required.

### Health & education
25. **What a primary health centre can—and cannot—do** — services, staffing, referral, geography, and patient experience.
26. **The economics of exam preparation** — household investment, coaching ecosystems, outcomes, and stress; carefully sourced.
27. **A school day in different geographies** — compare context, not create a simplistic ranking.

### Future & overlooked
28. **What will India's aging population mean for care?** — demography, family structures, work, and health systems.
29. **A small town's future after a new transport connection** — test assumptions using local evidence.
30. **The overlooked Indian invention or innovation** — tell the actual development story, including failures and collaborators.

These pitches are idea seeds. They should be narrowed to a specific question, location, evidence plan, and feasible narrative before selection.


## 12. Data and source infrastructure for the topic map

A topic map becomes more useful when research can quickly discover relevant evidence.

### Recommended source shelves

- **Census of India / Census Digital Library:** population, housing, literacy, workers, migration, language, administrative geography, census publications, tables, and population finder tools. Check year and limitations before using as current population truth.
- **data.gov.in:** government datasets across domains such as economy, education, environment, health, labor, transport, power, water, sanitation, tourism, and technology. Verify the dataset's owner, release date, definitions, completeness, and license.
- **MoSPI Compendium of Datasets and Registries:** useful discovery tool to find which ministry/agency produces a dataset, its subject, variables, and access route.
- **National Data Archive / Microdata Library:** discover survey metadata and unit-level datasets; check access restrictions, documentation, weights, and representativeness.
- **RBI publications:** banking, state-level economy, credit, prices, financial systems, and related statistical series.
- **State departments, municipal bodies, regulators, universities, research institutes, and archives:** for local and subject-specific evidence.
- **Primary reporting and interviews:** datasets cannot replace lived experience, and interviews cannot alone establish population-level claims.

### Research rule
The topic map is a discovery tool, not an evidence hierarchy. For each story, select sources based on the question. Official data can be incomplete, outdated, politically framed, or poorly suited to the claim; independent research can also have limitations. Record the provenance and limitations.

## 13. Editorial planning meeting (monthly, 45 minutes)

1. **What did we publish?** Review the last 4–8 stories and their category/format/location tags.
2. **What did we learn?** Audience behavior, reporting discoveries, production problems—not only page metrics.
3. **What is missing?** Notice geographic, subject, format, and perspective gaps.
4. **What is ready?** Move pitches into brief/research only if evidence and scope are credible.
5. **What is overloaded?** Check team capacity, tier, and long-lead stories.
6. **What should we stop?** Remove stale ideas, duplicate angles, or stories with no next action.
7. **One process change:** agree on one small improvement for next month.

Keep the agenda decision-oriented. Don't turn it into a large dashboard meeting.

---

## 14. The editorial decision principles

When two stories compete, prefer the one that:
- Has a sharper question
- Has a stronger evidence path
- Is less predictable in angle
- Can reveal something without overstating it
- Adds a missing dimension to the broader archive
- Has a credible human, place, system, or data anchor
- Can be told in the form best suited to its subject
- Is feasible without lowering standards

A story should **not** win merely because it is easier to make cinematic, more controversial, more patriotic, more negative, more viral, or more data-rich.

## 15. Recommended implementation for Viraaasat

### Create these planning assets
1. **India Topic Map** — this taxonomy, maintained as a living document.
2. **Pitch Backlog** — idea cards with status, tags, and next action.
3. **Coverage Tracker** — published stories tagged by subject, geography, form, time, evidence, and tier.
4. **Story Selection Scorecard** — weighted rubric above; retain the scores and short reasoning.
5. **Editorial Calendar** — only commissioned/committed stories, not every idea.
6. **Flagship Shelf** — ambitious long-lead stories with rough research requirements.

### Do not build a complex dashboard immediately
Start with a simple spreadsheet, GitHub Projects, or a structured Markdown file. A dashboard is only useful after enough stories exist to reveal real patterns.

### Proposed statuses
`Idea → Pitched → Selected → Briefing → Research → Writing → Editorial Review → Visual Plan → Production → Fact-check & QA → Ready → Published → Review`

Keep deferred/declined ideas with short rationale and revisit dates where useful.

## 16. What success looks like

After 12 published stories, Viraaasat should not be able to claim that it has “covered India.” But it should be able to answer:

- What kinds of India have we explored?
- Which geographies, communities, systems, and time periods have appeared repeatedly?
- What have we not yet examined?
- Which story forms have served our subjects best?
- What do our readers seem to understand or explore more deeply?
- Are we maintaining a consistent editorial identity without making every story feel the same?

The ambition is not completeness. It is **a growing, honest, interesting archive that keeps expanding the reader's picture of India**.

## 18. Final recommendation

Adopt the topic map now, but keep the publishing schedule flexible. The editorial system should create a **deliberate variety of questions**, not a mechanical variety of categories.

For the next planning session, choose **three initial story pitches**: one evergreen foundation, one distinctly local or regional story, and one surprising/overlooked angle. Score them, identify the evidence path, and select the one whose story can be told with the greatest clarity, integrity, and distinctive Viraaasat experience.

**Editorial principle to keep:** *Don't try to tell every story about India. Keep making the reader's picture of India larger.*
