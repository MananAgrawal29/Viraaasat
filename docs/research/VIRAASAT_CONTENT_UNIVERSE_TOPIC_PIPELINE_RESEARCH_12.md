# Research 12 — Viraaasat's Content Universe, Editorial Pillars & Topic Pipeline

**Project:** Viraaasat.in  
**Mission:** Define the editorial universe of Viraaasat, establish content pillars, identify high-value topic intersections, and create a repeatable system for selecting stories.  
**Status:** Research completed — recommendations, not final editorial commitments.


## 1. Executive conclusion

Viraaasat should not be a catalogue of “facts about India,” a travel website, a patriotic feed, or a news aggregation site. It should be an **interactive editorial publication that helps people understand India through carefully researched stories**.

The strongest editorial model is to organize coverage into broad pillars but commission individual stories around specific questions, tensions, changes, and human consequences. Pillars provide continuity; stories should not feel like category articles.

A useful positioning statement:

> **Viraaasat explores India—its people, places, systems, contradictions, past, and possible futures—one deeply researched story at a time.**

The publication should preserve complexity. It can celebrate, criticize, explain, investigate, question, or simply observe. It should never force a predetermined positive or negative conclusion.

## 2. Editorial principles

1. **Question-first:** Commission a question, not a topic label. “Water” is a topic; “Why do cities with monsoon rainfall still run short of water?” is a story question.
2. **Evidence before conclusion:** Gather evidence and context before deciding the story's thesis.
3. **Human scale:** Where appropriate, connect large systems and data to lived experience.
4. **India is not monolithic:** Show variation by state, district, region, language, class, gender, ecology, history, and urban/rural context. Avoid implying that one example represents the whole country.
5. **Good and bad are not quotas:** Do not force a positivity/negativity balance. Seek a fair, representative editorial range over time.
6. **Intersection stories:** Prefer stories that reveal relationships among systems—e.g., climate × migration, infrastructure × ecology, language × technology.
7. **Visuals have a job:** Maps, timelines, comparisons, charts, reconstructions, and motion should clarify evidence or create narrative meaning.
8. **No spectacle without substance:** Scroll effects and interactive experiences are storytelling tools, not the editorial product.
9. **Accessible by design:** Story information remains understandable without animation, heavy media, or interactive controls.
10. **Transparent uncertainty:** Explain definitions, missing data, limitations, and disagreements.

## 3. Editorial pillars

These pillars are a practical organizing system. A story may belong to more than one pillar; the primary category should be chosen for the archive and secondary tags should capture intersections.

### Pillar A — History & Civilizations

**Scope:** Ancient, medieval, early modern, colonial, post-independence, local histories, memory, archives, archaeology, historical institutions, historical myths and contested narratives.

Story directions:
- What did a historical trade route actually connect—and what moved along it besides goods?
- How did a city change across successive political eras?
- Which historical communities or people are missing from the popular narrative?
- What can an archaeological site tell us—and what cannot be inferred?
- How are monuments, museums, and archives deciding what gets remembered?
- The afterlife of a historical invention, institution, or idea.

Visual opportunities: map evolution, timeline, archival-image comparison, reconstructed spatial layouts, source annotations.

Editorial caution: separate primary evidence, scholarly interpretations, later legend, and modern political claims.

### Pillar B — Geography, Land & Environment

**Scope:** Landscapes, rivers, mountains, coasts, forests, biodiversity, climate, water, land use, natural hazards, ecological change.

Story directions:
- A river's journey from source to sea—and the people, cities, industries, and ecosystems along it.
- What happens to a wetland when a city expands around it?
- How heat exposure varies within one city.
- Why some landscapes are more vulnerable to floods, drought, landslides, or erosion.
- The ecology and livelihoods of a commons.
- The invisible geography of groundwater.

Visual opportunities: terrain, map layers, seasonal comparison, satellite/remote-sensing imagery, flow diagrams, climate charts.

Editorial caution: verify spatial scale, dates, definitions, climate attribution, and data uncertainty.

### Pillar C — Society, People & Everyday Life

**Scope:** Communities, identity, inequality, family, gender, caste, disability, labour, migration, demographics, everyday systems and lived experience.

Story directions:
- How does the same public service work differently for different people?
- What is changing in family structures, work, aspirations, or mobility?
- How does migration reshape the origin and destination communities?
- What does an official category or statistic conceal about people's experiences?
- How do ordinary people navigate informal systems?
- The realities behind a common social assumption.

Visual opportunities: carefully consented portraits, journeys, local maps, distribution charts, annotated narratives, oral history.

Editorial caution: informed consent, privacy, dignified portrayal, avoid tokenism and stereotyping, do not imply anecdotes prove prevalence.

### Pillar D — Politics, Governance & Institutions

**Scope:** Constitution, elections, federalism, Parliament, courts, local governance, public administration, policy, public accountability, political institutions.

Story directions:
- What happens after a law or policy is announced?
- How does a decision move from national policy to a local outcome?
- What does a municipal body actually control?
- How do constituency boundaries, representation, and electoral geography shape outcomes?
- How does a public grievance system work in practice?
- What is the institutional history behind a major public service?

Visual opportunities: process diagrams, jurisdiction maps, budget flows, timelines, election data, explanatory interactions.

Editorial caution: distinguish official claims, measured outcomes, political arguments, and independently verified evidence; maintain neutral language and consistent standards.

### Pillar E — Economy, Work & Inequality

**Scope:** Employment, labour, informal economy, household finances, prices, business, manufacturing, trade, financial inclusion, public spending, economic geography.

Story directions:
- What does a headline economic indicator mean for a household?
- How does a product travel from raw material to consumer?
- What changes when a town becomes an industrial cluster?
- Where do jobs exist, and how do job quality and participation vary?
- What is the hidden labour behind everyday convenience?
- How do household costs vary across places and income groups?

Visual opportunities: Sankey/flow diagrams, price baskets, supply-chain maps, time series, district comparisons, small multiples.

Editorial caution: explain nominal vs real, definitions, denominators, time windows, informal work measurement, and causation.

### Pillar F — Science, Technology & Innovation

**Scope:** Research, space, medicine, engineering, digital infrastructure, AI, startups, scientific institutions, technological history, innovation beyond headline companies.

Story directions:
- What does a major scientific mission actually do—and what does it not do?
- How does a technology reach users outside the lab?
- The people and infrastructure behind a seemingly simple digital service.
- What does AI change for a particular profession, language, or community?
- How does a research institution turn discoveries into public benefit?
- The overlooked engineering behind an Indian system.

Visual opportunities: mechanism explainers, diagrams, simulations, mission timelines, scale comparisons, interactive models.

Editorial caution: avoid hype, verify technical details with experts and primary sources, clearly label simulations and conceptual reconstructions.

### Pillar G — Culture, Language & Identity

**Scope:** Languages, literature, music, cinema, craft, food, fashion, performance, rituals, popular culture, regional aesthetics, cultural change.

Story directions:
- How does a language change when its speakers migrate?
- The story behind a craft—and the labour, economics, and knowledge sustaining it.
- How a musical form travels between communities and generations.
- Food as ecology, trade, migration, class, and identity.
- What a film genre reveals about a particular moment in society.
- How traditions change when they move into cities, online spaces, or new markets.

Visual opportunities: audio, oral history, process photography, maps of cultural circulation, annotated objects, short clips with rights clearance.

Editorial caution: avoid flattening a living culture into an exotic exhibit; acknowledge regional and internal variation; verify provenance and media rights.

### Pillar H — Cities, Infrastructure & the Built World

**Scope:** Urbanization, housing, roads, railways, ports, public transport, sanitation, electricity, public spaces, architecture, planning, construction, rural infrastructure.

Story directions:
- How does a city move millions of people every day?
- The transformation of a neighbourhood through a new transport line.
- What happens to a street when its purpose changes?
- How do housing affordability and commute time interact?
- Why do some public spaces work while others fail?
- The lifecycle of a major infrastructure project—from promise to usage.

Visual opportunities: before/after maps, network diagrams, transit maps, cross-sections, street-level storytelling, spatial comparisons.

Editorial caution: avoid confusing proposed infrastructure with completed or operational projects; clearly label plans, timelines, and budget status.

### Pillar I — Conflict, Crisis & Difficult Truths

**Scope:** Conflict, communal tension, violence, disasters, failures, injustice, institutional breakdown, misinformation, difficult or uncomfortable histories.

Story directions:
- How did a crisis unfold—and what systems shaped the outcome?
- What can the evidence tell us about a disputed event?
- How does a disaster affect communities long after the news cycle ends?
- What failed, what worked, and what changed afterward?
- How are narratives around a difficult past formed and contested?

Visual opportunities: verified timelines, geographic context, evidence-led diagrams, restrained data presentation, archival documentation.

Editorial caution: high-sensitivity review, corroboration, trauma-aware reporting, careful terminology, avoid sensational imagery and false equivalence.

### Pillar J — Sport & Collective Culture

**Scope:** Sport beyond match results: institutions, participation, economics, geography, fandom, access, gender, training, infrastructure, history.

Story directions:
- Why is a sport popular in one region and less accessible in another?
- The pathway from grassroots participation to elite performance.
- The economics and infrastructure behind a sport.
- The story of a local sporting ecosystem.
- How gender, class, disability, or geography shape access.

Visual opportunities: maps, participation data, timelines, field diagrams, profiles, short documentary sequences.

Editorial caution: distinguish participation from professional success; verify statistics and competition context.

### Pillar K — India & the World

**Scope:** India's connections with global trade, migration, diaspora, diplomacy, science, climate, culture, technology, and geopolitics.

Story directions:
- How does a global supply chain connect Indian workers, consumers, and businesses?
- What does migration change on both sides?
- India's role in an international scientific or technological collaboration.
- How a global event affects a local Indian community or industry.
- How cultural exports travel and change abroad.

Visual opportunities: routes, networks, comparison maps, timelines, trade flows, diaspora stories.

Editorial caution: avoid simplistic national comparisons; align definitions, dates, and units.

### Pillar L — The Future & What Comes Next

**Scope:** Long-term trends, scenarios, emerging technologies, demographic change, future cities, climate adaptation, education, work, energy, space.

Story directions:
- What could a particular Indian city look like in 2040 under different assumptions?
- What choices shape the future of a river, coastline, or region?
- How might work change with automation or new technologies?
- What are the plausible pathways for energy, transport, or urban systems?
- What is already changing—and what remains uncertain?

Visual opportunities: scenario sliders, interactive models, future timelines, maps, systems diagrams.

Editorial caution: scenarios are not forecasts; state assumptions, uncertainty, and limits.

## 4. Editorial balance dashboard

Do not impose fixed weekly quotas. Review the catalogue over a rolling period (e.g., 8–12 stories) to detect concentration and blind spots.

Suggested editorial dimensions to track:
- Primary pillar
- Secondary pillar(s)
- Geographic coverage: national, region, state, district, local
- Time focus: past, present, future
- Story mode: explainer, investigation, profile, visual essay, interactive, data story, oral history
- Emotional register: wonder, concern, curiosity, tension, intimacy, surprise, reflection
- Evidence mode: archival, data, reporting, expert analysis, observation, oral testimony
- Coverage orientation: people, systems, place, institution, idea
- Audience entry point: known question, surprising reveal, overlooked story, debate, personal connection

The dashboard is for reflection—not a formula. It should identify underrepresented areas, not force a topic to fit a quota.

## 6. Story prioritization framework

Score a candidate story from 1–5 on each criterion. Use the scores to guide discussion, not to automatically select the highest total.

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Editorial value | Does it reveal something important, meaningful, or misunderstood? |
| Originality | Can Viraaasat add a distinct perspective instead of repeating existing coverage? |
| Evidence availability | Are credible sources, data, experts, records, or reporting opportunities accessible? |
| Visual potential | Do visual forms materially improve understanding? |
| Narrative potential | Is there a compelling question, progression, tension, or reveal? |
| Scope | Can a small team produce a rigorous story within a realistic timeframe? |
| Human relevance | Does it connect to people's lives, decisions, systems, or place? |
| Timeliness or evergreen value | Is there a reason now, or will it remain valuable over time? |
| Sensitivity & risk | Can the team responsibly report it with available time and safeguards? |

**Suggested decision:** Stories scoring well on editorial value, evidence, narrative, and feasibility should move forward. A story with high spectacle but weak evidence or unmanageable scope should be deferred or narrowed.

## 7. Content formats to use across pillars

The topic does not dictate the format. Choose format after the story question and evidence are clear.

- **Visual explainer:** one core mechanism or concept; diagrams and clear progression.
- **Timeline story:** change over time, sequence, turning points, consequences.
- **Map story:** geography is essential to understanding.
- **Data story:** distribution, comparison, trends, relationships, uncertainty.
- **Documentary profile:** a person, community, institution, place, or practice.
- **Archive story:** images/documents with provenance and contextual interpretation.
- **Interactive comparison:** reader selects places, periods, categories, or scenarios.
- **Scrollytelling essay:** authored sequence where the scroll controls narrative pacing.
- **Exploration mode:** reader-led browsing when the subject benefits from discovery.
- **Hybrid:** a carefully composed combination of formats.

## 8. Recurring editorial series concepts

These are optional repeatable series identities, not permanent content silos.

- **The Long View:** slow, layered historical stories.
- **Lines on the Map:** geography, borders, routes, regions, and spatial inequality.
- **Under the Surface:** systems and infrastructure most people don't see.
- **Everyday India:** a close look at familiar daily experiences.
- **Numbers, Human:** data grounded in lived context.
- **A Different Lens:** challenge an assumption with evidence and nuance.
- **The Archive:** historical documents, objects, photographs, and memory.
- **After the Headline:** long-term consequences after a news moment.
- **Possible Futures:** scenario stories about choices and uncertainty.
- **Made in India (broadly interpreted):** the people, process, science, and systems behind things created here.

Use a series label only when it adds reader recognition. Do not force a story into one.

## 9. Geographic coverage strategy

The archive should gradually build a sense of India's geographic breadth without tokenistically assigning each state a story.

Track:
- State and Union Territory represented
- District or local scale when relevant
- Urban, peri-urban, rural, remote, or mixed context
- Region (North, South, East, West, Central, Northeast—recognizing these are broad and sometimes imperfect groupings)
- Physical geography (mountains, coast, desert, plains, plateau, islands, river basin)
- Language(s) and local sources used

Review geographic distribution over time. Local stories should have local expertise, sourcing, and context; location alone is not a diversity checkbox.

## 10. Research-source starting points

Use primary sources and specialist reporting wherever possible. These are source discovery starting points, not automatic endorsements or proof.

- **MoSPI and National Statistical Office:** official statistical releases, household surveys, national accounts, labour, prices, consumption, and other datasets.
- **Census of India:** population, demographic, and administrative data; note release status and vintage.
- **RBI Database on Indian Economy (DBIE):** financial, macroeconomic, and banking data.
- **NFHS:** health, nutrition, gender, and household indicators.
- **UDISE+ / AISHE:** education system statistics.
- **ASER:** foundational learning and rural education surveys.
- **NCRB:** recorded crime and related official statistics, with caveats about reporting and classification.
- **Election Commission of India:** election data, constituencies, results, and official records.
- **India WRIS / Central Water Commission / relevant state agencies:** water data and hydrological information, depending on subject.
- **IMD / CPCB / CEA:** weather, air/environment, energy data as relevant.
- **CAG and budget documents:** public spending, audit, and accountability material.
- **Academic research and repositories:** scholarly context, methods, and historical evidence.
- **Independent specialist publishers and reporting:** leads, context, field reporting, and alternate perspectives; verify key factual claims independently.

For every dataset, check publication date, definition, coverage, geography, denominator, collection method, revision history, missingness, and whether comparisons are valid.

## 11. Publishing cadence and small-team sustainability

With a three-person team and a weekly publishing ambition, use a rolling pipeline instead of having all three people start each story from scratch.

**Pipeline stages:**
1. Idea pool
2. Candidate review
3. Commissioned
4. Researching
5. Narrative outline
6. Draft & source review
7. Story design/build
8. QA
9. Scheduled
10. Published
11. Updated / corrected / archived

Maintain several stories at different stages. A weekly rhythm is an aspiration; it should not force weak evidence or overwork. If the story needs more time, publish when ready and communicate honestly.

## 12. Recommended initial editorial mix

For the first 8–12 published stories, aim for a deliberately varied set of experiences rather than a quota by pillar. A possible mix:
- 2 stories with history, memory, or culture as a central lens
- 2 stories grounded in geography, ecology, water, or climate
- 2 stories on people, society, work, or everyday life
- 1 story explaining an institution, policy, or system
- 1 story about science, technology, or infrastructure
- 1 story with a difficult, contested, or overlooked issue
- 1 story looking at an India–world connection or future scenario

This is a sample editorial season, not a requirement. One story may cover several of these lenses.

## 14. Decisions to adopt for Viraaasat

1. Adopt the twelve pillars as an internal taxonomy, not visible rigid sections that dictate the homepage.
2. Tag each story with one primary pillar and a small set of secondary tags.
3. Build an idea backlog using question-shaped story pitches.
4. Use the prioritization rubric for editorial discussion.
5. Review coverage every 8–12 stories to identify blind spots.
6. Start with a curated set of stories, not an endless feed.
7. Treat recurring series as optional labels that emerge from the work.
8. Make evidence, narrative, visual potential, and production feasibility all necessary for commissioning.
9. Maintain the editorial mix without artificial positive/negative quotas.
10. Keep the weekly cadence sustainable and allow stories to take longer when needed.

## 16. Final editorial philosophy

Viraaasat should not ask, “What is interesting about India today?” and settle for the first answer.

It should ask:
- What is misunderstood?
- What is invisible?
- What has changed?
- What is still changing?
- Who benefits?
- Who bears the cost?
- What evidence can we find?
- What can a reader see, compare, or experience that they could not before?

The goal is not to tell every story about India. It is to tell **specific stories that help readers understand India more deeply**.

> **One story a week. One question worth asking. India, in all its complexity.**
