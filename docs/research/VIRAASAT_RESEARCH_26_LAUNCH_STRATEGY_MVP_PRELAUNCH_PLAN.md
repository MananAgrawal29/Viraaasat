# Viraaasat Research 26 — Launch Strategy, Minimum Viable Publication & Pre-launch Plan

**Mission:** Convert Viraaasat's research and identity into a launchable publication without endless preparation or a generic MVP.

## Executive conclusion

Launch the smallest version of Viraaasat that still feels unmistakably like Viraaasat: a complete, trustworthy publication with one exceptional story and the essential editorial, technical, and audience infrastructure. Do not launch a generic blog, but do not let flagship-level ambitions delay publishing indefinitely.

## 1. Launch definition

The launch is successful when a new reader can:
1. Understand what Viraaasat is and why it exists.
2. Find and read the first story on a reliable, responsive website.
3. Navigate the archive (even if it initially contains only one story and a clearly labeled upcoming/empty state).
4. Understand the story's evidence, sources, media credits, and uncertainty.
5. Share the story and receive a deliberate social preview.
6. Subscribe or otherwise choose a clear way to return, if newsletter infrastructure is ready.
7. Contact the team, submit a correction, or send feedback through a simple, safe channel.

Team must be able to publish, monitor, correct, and maintain the site without heroic effort.


## 2. MVP scope

### Must ship
- Brand identity and clear About/mission page.
- Homepage with a cinematic opening that quickly explains the publication and presents the featured story.
- Story template with title, deck, author/byline, reading time/date, chapter structure, narrative scenes, source list, credits, and related/next-story treatment.
- At least one fully produced story; a second story is strongly recommended if feasible, but not a launch blocker if the first is excellent.
- Archive index with clear category or metadata; avoid pretending there is a large archive.
- Responsive mobile experience and readable fallback if motion/JS is reduced or unavailable.
- Keyboard navigation, focus states, text alternatives, reduced-motion behavior, sensible contrast, and accessible controls.
- SEO essentials: unique titles/descriptions, canonical URLs, sitemap, robots policy, Open Graph/social metadata, and indexable production pages.
- HTTPS and secured account/domain access; backups and a recovery procedure.
- Privacy statement reflecting actual data collection; contact/correction channel.
- Analytics at a privacy-conscious minimal level, configured and tested, if used.
- Production deployment and monitoring owner; error reporting/feedback path.

### Should ship if ready
- Newsletter signup and first issue.
- A second story to show repeatability.
- One signature interactive (map, timeline, chart, comparison) that materially improves the first story.
- Social package for launch story: carousel, vertical short, story frames.
- Editorial corrections policy and visible correction mechanism.

### Post-launch
- Multiple languages and localization.
- Public comments, accounts, reader profiles, open uploads, forums.
- Advanced search, recommendation engine, complex CMS.
- Large-scale 3D on the homepage; only add where story-specific and justified.
- Push notifications, gamification, memberships, community platform.
- Large social channel portfolio or daily content cadence.
- Complex dashboards, experiments, personalization, or tracking.

## 3. The first-story rule

The first story must demonstrate the editorial promise—not every technical capability. Choose a subject with:
- a compelling question or tension;
- clear, verifiable evidence;
- a strong visual spine (places, people, archives, maps, data, or process);
- manageable scope for the three-person team;
- a meaningful reveal or insight;
- enough narrative material for a complete experience;
- realistic rights clearance;
- an achievable mobile version.

Avoid choosing the first story solely because it is easy to animate, currently trending, or emotionally provocative. First-story production should calibrate the pipeline, not set an unsustainable bar.

## 4. First 4–8 stories: portfolio, not a rigid schedule

Plan the first 4 stories as a balanced opening portfolio, then hold stories 5–8 as a flexible horizon. The portfolio should span different lenses rather than four near-identical topics.

Possible balance dimensions:
- geography: north/south/east/west/central/northeast, rural, urban, small cities, border and coastal contexts;
- subject: history, society, science, culture, economy, infrastructure, environment, politics, everyday life;
- scale: individual, local, regional, national, global;
- time: historical, present, future-facing;
- emotional register: wonder, reflection, complexity, urgency, hope, grief—but never manufactured;
- format: editorial, data-driven, map-led, timeline, photo/archive, interactive comparison.

This is a planning heuristic, not a quota. Do not force representational categories into the first four stories. Track imbalance over time.

## 5. Pre-launch timeline (suggested 3–4 weeks, adaptable)

### Week 1 — Freeze scope and prepare
- Confirm launch story topic, question, scope, owner, tier, publication target.
- Complete research, source/claim ledger, draft, and narrative outline.
- Lock homepage and story information architecture.
- Identify all media and confirm rights/permissions/credits.
- Define must-have launch features and deferred backlog.
- Write About, privacy, correction/contact, and editorial standards pages.

### Week 2 — Build and integrate
- Implement foundational pages and story scenes.
- Build story-specific interaction(s) and static alternatives.
- Integrate media with responsive crops, alt text, credits, captions, and optimization.
- Prepare social assets and newsletter draft, if planned.
- Establish production analytics and error/feedback path.

### Week 3 — Review and test
- Editorial review by a teammate who did not author the draft.
- Verify claims, quotes, numbers, captions, image context, and citations.
- Verify licensing, consent, and permissions.
- Test desktop, mobile, tablet, narrow viewport, and landscape.
- Test keyboard-only navigation, focus, screen-reader basics, zoom/reflow, reduced motion.
- Test performance and media-loading behavior on throttled/slow connection.
- Test all links, controls, navigation, form submissions, and error states.
- Test social previews and metadata.
- Use staging/preview with realistic content; fix blockers.

### Week 4 — Launch preparation and release
- Freeze avoidable changes before launch.
- Run final production-build check and crawl.
- Confirm domain, HTTPS, canonical host, robots, sitemap, analytics, and contact route.
- Verify backup and recovery procedure.
- Prepare launch copy, social posts, newsletter, and distribution schedule.
- Agree on go/no-go and who is responsible for post-launch monitoring.
- Publish; run live smoke tests before promotion.
- Announce only after live verification.

A shorter runway is possible, but preserve the order: content and scope, risk testing, cutover preparation, live verification, then promotion.

## 6. Release gates (must pass)

### Gate A — Editorial ready
- Story question, thesis/central insight, scope, and audience takeaway are clear.
- Claims are checked; uncertainties and disputed interpretations are presented honestly.
- Sources are accessible and cited.
- Numbers have units, timeframe, denominator, definitions, and limitations where needed.
- Story is reviewed by someone other than its primary author.

### Gate B — Rights ready
- All images, video, audio, fonts, maps, and data have verified usage status.
- Credits and attribution are complete.
- Consent/privacy risks have been reviewed.
- No unlicensed or unknown assets are used as factual documentary evidence.

### Gate C — Experience ready
- Reading flow works without animation.
- Motion respects reduced-motion preferences.
- Mobile experience is intentionally adapted.
- Interactive elements have understandable controls and text alternatives.
- No scroll hijacking or trapped reading sequences.

### Gate D — Technical ready
- Production build succeeds.
- No critical console/runtime errors on primary journeys.
- HTTPS, canonical URL, robots and sitemap are correct.
- Critical links and controls work.
- Media loading and responsive behavior are tested.
- No secrets exposed in source or client bundle.
- Backup/recovery is known and tested or rehearsed.

### Gate E — Operational ready
- A launch owner and monitoring owner are named.
- Feedback/correction channel is reachable.
- Team knows how to fix and redeploy.
- Launch message and distribution assets are prepared.
- Team has capacity for post-launch review and fixes.

## 7. QA checklist

### Content and trust
- [ ] No placeholder copy, sample content, TODOs, dummy links, or demo images.
- [ ] Names, dates, facts, quotes, statistics, captions, and sources proofread.
- [ ] Story reviewed independently.
- [ ] Credits, permissions, privacy and correction route visible.

### Navigation and interaction
- [ ] Logo and navigation work.
- [ ] All internal/external links checked.
- [ ] Buttons and interactive modules work by mouse, touch, and keyboard.
- [ ] No broken/empty states or confusing dead ends.
- [ ] 404 page useful and returns the proper status.

### Responsive and accessibility
- [ ] Mobile-first review on real phone if possible.
- [ ] Tablet and desktop checked.
- [ ] Keyboard navigation and visible focus.
- [ ] Text zoom/reflow checked.
- [ ] Color contrast checked.
- [ ] Alt text/captions/transcripts provided as appropriate.
- [ ] Reduced-motion and non-motion experience checked.
- [ ] Screen-reader spot-check of core paths.

### Performance
- [ ] Hero/LCP asset appropriately sized and prioritized.
- [ ] Below-fold media lazy-loaded.
- [ ] Images responsive and aspect ratio reserved to reduce layout shift.
- [ ] Fonts limited and loading strategy checked.
- [ ] Heavy map/3D/animation code loaded only when needed.
- [ ] Slow-network behavior tested.

### Search and sharing
- [ ] Unique title and description for each public page.
- [ ] Canonical URL correct.
- [ ] Sitemap includes only canonical/indexable URLs.
- [ ] robots.txt does not block public pages unintentionally.
- [ ] Open Graph and social preview tested.
- [ ] No staging noindex directive left on production.

### Security and operations
- [ ] MFA on critical accounts; team access reviewed.
- [ ] Secrets kept out of repository and public client bundle.
- [ ] Dependencies reviewed and lockfile maintained.
- [ ] HTTPS works and redirects are correct.
- [ ] Backup and recovery notes available.
- [ ] Contact/correction forms have spam and abuse protections appropriate to their risk.
- [ ] Monitoring and post-launch owner established.

## 8. Launch day sequence

1. Final release decision from the team.
2. Confirm release commit/build and backups.
3. Publish/deploy.
4. Smoke-test live site: homepage, story, navigation, images, interactive, contact/subscribe, sharing preview, mobile.
5. Confirm robots, sitemap, analytics and error monitoring.
6. Only after the live smoke-test, post the public announcement.
7. Share the story in planned channels; avoid posting everywhere simultaneously if the team cannot respond.
8. Keep a short issue log; triage critical problems first.

## 9. First 14 days after launch

### First 24 hours
- Check availability, errors, broken links/media, forms, and reader feedback.
- Fix critical defects immediately; document non-critical ones.
- Do not make editorial decisions based on an early noisy traffic spike.

### Days 2–7
- Review discoverability, referrals, newsletter signups, shares, story progression, interaction use, and mobile/performance issues.
- Ask a small number of readers for qualitative feedback: What was confusing? What surprised you? What would you share?
- Avoid adding features in response to one comment unless the issue is clearly important.

### Days 8–14
- Hold a 15–20 minute retrospective.
- Record what slowed production, what was overbuilt, what quality checks caught issues, and which audience signals were useful.
- Choose one improvement for story 2—not a full redesign.

## 10. Launch messaging

### Main announcement concept
“India is not one story. We’re here to look closer.”

This is a possible launch line, not a tagline lock. Use a concise statement of the mission, link to the first story, and an invitation to explore or subscribe. Avoid claiming the site is the definitive source or represents every Indian experience.

### Distribution package
- Website: complete experience.
- Instagram carousel: 5–8 frames; question, context, key insight, evidence, invitation.
- Short video: 20–45 seconds as a test; create from one story facet, not an entire story compressed.
- Stories: 2–4 frames if useful.
- Newsletter: personal note + story + one notable detail + return invitation.
- Direct outreach: send thoughtfully to people likely to care; don't mass-spam.

## 11. Success measures for the first launch

Use a small learning dashboard, not vanity targets:
- Website reliability and critical errors.
- Story visits and source/referral patterns.
- Returning visitors (aggregate/privacy-conscious).
- Newsletter signups, if offered.
- Shares/saves and meaningful replies.
- Chapter progression and interactive completion as rough indicators only—not comprehension.
- Qualitative feedback: clarity, credibility, emotional/narrative impact, mobile experience.
- Production effort and hours per story format.

Do not set a rigid traffic target before establishing baseline audience and distribution capacity. A first launch is primarily an experiment in publication quality and repeatability.

## 12. Recommended team split for launch

- **Editorial & research lead:** story scope, source ledger, draft, fact-check coordination, editorial proof.
- **Visual & experience lead:** art direction, scene design, motion, media/rights, visual proof.
- **Technical & production lead:** architecture, implementation, performance, testing, deployment, technical QA.
- All three: release gate, launch decision, post-publish review. Role assignments may overlap, but no creator should be the sole reviewer of their own high-risk work.

## 13. Scope-cut order when time runs out

Cut or postpone in this order:
1. Decorative motion that adds no narrative meaning.
2. Secondary interactions and additional data views.
3. Extra social formats beyond the core package.
4. Additional stories in the archive that are not ready.
5. Complex homepage 3D or expensive visual effects.

Do **not** cut:
- accuracy and context;
- source quality and citations;
- rights and permission checks;
- essential accessibility;
- mobile usability;
- security and privacy basics;
- functional navigation and critical user paths.

## 14. A practical definition of “ready”

Ready does not mean perfect. It means:
- the central story is strong;
- the site delivers its promise;
- known risks have been addressed;
- no critical defects remain;
- deferred items are written down;
- the team can maintain the release;
- there is a next story in the pipeline.
